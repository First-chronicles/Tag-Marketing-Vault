A post-monsoon plumbing checklist for Mesa AZ homes covers seven things: outdoor drains, slab leak warning signs, a water heater flush, water pressure, the water softener, every shutoff valve, and the fixtures nobody used all summer. Most of it takes an afternoon, and catching a problem now is far cheaper than catching it in January.

September is a strange month for plumbing in the East Valley. The storms are tapering off, the heat is still hanging on, and most homeowners are focused on getting through the last stretch of triple digits rather than on what is happening under the sink. That is exactly why this checklist exists.

Monsoon storms move a lot of dirt and debris into drain lines, and a summer of nonstop demand is hard on water heaters. The problems that show up in October and November usually started in July and August. Here is what we recommend checking before the season turns.

The 7-point checklist at a glance

  1. Clear storm debris out of outdoor drains
  2. Check for slab leak warning signs
  3. Flush the water heater
  4. Test your water pressure
  5. Service the water softener
  6. Exercise every shutoff valve
  7. Run the fixtures you never use

1. Clear storm debris out of outdoor drains

Monsoon rain pushes dirt, leaves and gravel into places it does not belong. Walk your property and look at:

  • Yard and patio drains
  • Driveway channel drains
  • Downspout outlets and any line that carries roof water away from the house

If water pools instead of draining, the line is probably partially blocked. A garden hose test is the quickest way to check: run water into the drain for a minute and watch whether it clears or backs up.

If it backs up, skip the chemical drain cleaner. It rarely moves a debris clog and it is hard on older pipes. A camera inspection shows exactly where the blockage sits, which is the difference between clearing one section and digging up a yard. Our drain cleaning service in Mesa covers both.

Storm season also pushes debris and root growth into main sewer lines, which is a bigger problem than a yard drain. If you saw any backup during the summer, read what monsoon storms do to sewer lines before you write it off.

2. Check for slab leak warning signs

Slab leaks are one of the most common problems we handle in Mesa, Gilbert, Chandler and Queen Creek. Local soil shifts with heat and heavy rain, and that movement puts steady stress on the water lines running under the foundation.

Watch for:

  • A water bill that jumped without a change in usage
  • Warm spots on tile or concrete floors
  • The sound of running water when every fixture is off
  • New cracks in flooring or baseboards
  • A mildew smell with no obvious source

Two or more of these together is worth a call. Slab leaks do not resolve on their own, and a small leak in September can become a foundation problem by winter. The EPA notes that household leaks waste enormous volumes of water nationwide, and a slab leak runs constantly and silently (EPA, Fix a Leak Week).

3. Flush the water heater

Your water heater has been working all summer, and Arizona hard water leaves a layer of mineral sediment in the bottom of the tank. That sediment makes the unit run longer to hit the same temperature, drives up the energy bill, and shortens the life of the tank.

September is the right time to flush it, before you start leaning on hot water in October and November. If you have a tankless unit, a descaling service does the same job.

While you are down there, check the age. Tank-style heaters generally last 8 to 12 years, and hard water tends to push East Valley units toward the lower end of that range. If yours is close, it is better to plan a replacement than to discover the problem mid-shower. 4. Test your water pressure

High water pressure is a quiet pipe killer. When pressure sits above 80 PSI, fixtures, supply lines, washing machine hoses and water heaters all wear out faster than they should, and small leaks start showing up at connections.

A pressure gauge from any hardware store threads onto an outdoor hose bib and gives you a reading in seconds. Above 80 PSI means your pressure reducing valve is failing, or the house never had one. Both are fixable in a single visit.

5. Service the water softener

If you have a softener, check three things:

  • Salt level. Keep the brine tank topped up.
  • Salt bridging. Break up any hard crust that has formed across the top of the salt.
  • Actual performance. Spotting on dishes, dry skin after a shower, or scale returning at the faucet all suggest the system is not keeping up.

Arizona sits at the hard end of the national scale for water hardness, which is why so many East Valley homes replace fixtures and water heaters earlier than expected (USGS, hardness of water). 6. Exercise every shutoff valve

Find the main shutoff and every fixture shutoff in the house, then turn each one off and back on. Valves that have not moved in years tend to seize, and the worst possible time to learn that is during a burst supply line at 2 a.m.

Make a note of any valve that will not turn, turns but does not stop the water, or drips after you move it. Those are quick fixes now and emergencies later.

While you are at it, make sure everyone in the house knows where the main shutoff is.

7. Run the fixtures you never use

Guest bathrooms, utility sinks, laundry sinks and outdoor showers develop dry P-traps over a long hot summer. Once the trap dries out, there is nothing blocking sewer gas from coming back into the room.

Run each one for a full minute to refill the trap. If you still smell sewer gas afterward, that points to a venting or drain issue rather than a dry trap, and it is worth having looked at.

Frequently asked questions

What plumbing problems does monsoon season cause in Mesa?

Monsoon storms wash dirt, gravel and organic debris into yard drains, downspout lines and sewer laterals, which causes slow drainage and backups weeks after the storm. Heavy rain also shifts soil around foundations and buried pipe, which is a common trigger for slab leaks and sewer line separation in the East Valley.

How often should I flush a water heater in Arizona?

Once a year at minimum, and September is a practical time to do it. Arizona's hard water builds sediment faster than in most of the country, so homes without a water softener often benefit from flushing every six to nine months. Tankless units need a descaling service on a similar schedule.

What water pressure is too high for a house?

Anything above 80 PSI is considered too high and is the point where plumbing codes generally require a pressure reducing valve. Sustained high pressure shortens the life of fixtures, supply lines, appliance hoses and water heaters, and it makes small leaks appear at joints and connections.

How do I know if I have a slab leak?

The most common signs are an unexplained jump in the water bill, warm spots on the floor, the sound of running water with every fixture off, new cracks in flooring, and a persistent mildew smell. Any two of those together justify a professional leak detection visit, because a slab leak runs continuously until it is found.

Is fall a good time for a plumbing inspection in the East Valley?

Yes. Fall catches the damage monsoon season caused while the weather is still comfortable to work in, and it puts the water heater in good shape before the months when you actually use it. It is also easier to get scheduled in the fall than during a January cold snap.
