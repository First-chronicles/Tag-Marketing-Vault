A water softener works quietly in the garage until the day it stops. Then the spots come back on the glass, the soap stops lathering, and the scale starts building again in your water heater.

Most of that is avoidable. Water softener maintenance takes a few minutes a month and one proper check a year. Here is what to look at in an Arizona home, where the water is hard enough that a neglected softener shows it fast.

Why maintenance matters more with Arizona water

The City of Mesa reports hardness between 12 and 22 grains per gallon, depending on which water source serves your area. That is very hard water, and the harder the water, the harder your softener works every day.

A softener running at the top of that range uses more salt, regenerates more often and puts more wear on its parts than the same unit in a soft-water state. The city's own advice is simple: follow the manufacturer's recommendations and service the unit regularly.

Your monthly check: salt level and salt type

Lift the lid on the brine tank once a month and look at the salt.

  • Keep it topped up, but not packed. A common rule of thumb is to keep the tank at least a quarter full and not much past halfway. Your owner's manual has the exact level for your model.
  • The salt should sit above the water. If you can see standing water over the salt, the tank is low or something is wrong.
  • Use the right salt. Evaporated pellets are the cleanest option. Rock salt costs less but leaves more insoluble residue, which settles as sludge at the bottom of the tank.
  • Potassium is an option. The City of Mesa notes that some softeners use potassium instead of sodium. Check that your unit supports it before switching.

How often you add salt depends on your hardness, your household size and how much water you use. A family in a high-hardness area will refill more often than a couple in a lower one. Checking monthly is what keeps you from finding out the hard way.

How to spot and fix a salt bridge

A salt bridge is a hard crust that forms across the salt, leaving an empty space underneath. The system keeps running, but it can no longer make the brine it needs to regenerate. Your water slowly turns hard again while the tank looks full.

Signs of a salt bridge:

  • The salt level has not dropped in weeks, even though you use water normally.
  • Hard water symptoms are back: spotting, scale and poor lather.
  • Pressing down on the salt with a broom handle meets a solid layer.

What to do: gently tap and break the crust with a broom handle, then scoop out the loose chunks. Do not use anything sharp or heavy that could crack the tank. If the crust is thick or keeps coming back, overfilling, humidity and low-quality salt are the usual causes.

A related problem is salt mushing, where fine salt sludge settles at the bottom and clogs the brine intake. That one usually means draining and cleaning the brine tank.

Regeneration: how often, and when to adjust it

Regeneration is the cycle where the softener flushes the collected minerals out of the resin and recharges it with brine. How often it runs depends on the system's capacity, your water hardness and your daily water use.

There are two common types of control:

  • Metered (demand) valves track water use and regenerate when the resin needs it. These adjust on their own.
  • Timer valves regenerate on a fixed schedule, whether you used the water or not.

Two Arizona-specific things are worth checking:

  1. Is the hardness setting right? Grains per gallon is the unit used to set a softener, and Mesa's water ranges from 12 to 22 gpg depending on the source. If you bought the house with the softener already installed, the setting may reflect the previous owner's guess. Set too low, hard water slips through. Set too high, you waste salt and water.
  2. Did the clock survive the last storm? Summer monsoon storms can knock out power. On some timer-based units, an outage resets the clock, so the softener starts regenerating in the middle of the day instead of overnight.

If your softener seems to be regenerating constantly, or not at all, that is worth a closer look.

The yearly check

Once a year, look beyond the salt:

  • Clean the brine tank when the salt is low, removing sludge and old residue.
  • Replace the sediment prefilter if your system has one.
  • Look at the resin. Resin lasts many years, but iron and chlorine can foul or wear it down. Some manufacturers recommend a resin cleaner on a set schedule.
  • Check the bypass valve, the drain line and the connections for leaks, kinks or mineral crust.
  • Confirm the irrigation line bypasses the softener. The City of Mesa advises connecting drip irrigation and landscape watering upstream of the softener, because the added sodium can harm plants.

A plumber can also check the control valve and injector, test your water before and after the softener, and confirm the settings match your home. That fits naturally into a wider plumbing maintenance visit.

Signs your softener needs service

  • Spotting on dishes and shower glass is back.
  • Soap and shampoo do not lather the way they did.
  • The salt level never drops, or drops much faster than usual.
  • Standing water in the brine tank.
  • Water tastes salty.
  • Water pressure has dropped since the softener was installed.
  • The system runs or makes noise at odd hours.

Any of these can mean a salt bridge, a clogged injector, the wrong settings or worn resin. Some are quick fixes, and some mean the unit is near the end of its life. The signs of hard water in your home are usually the first clue.

What about salt-free systems?

Salt-free conditioners do not need salt, a brine tank or regeneration. Their maintenance is different: the treatment media is replaced on a schedule, and the prefilter needs changing. If you are still choosing between the two, see salt-based vs salt-free water softeners in Arizona.

Frequently asked questions

How often should I add salt to my water softener?

It depends on your water hardness, household size and water use. Check the brine tank monthly and top it up when it drops toward a quarter full. Homes with harder water and bigger households will refill more often.

How do I know if my water softener has a salt bridge?

The most common sign is a salt level that stays the same for weeks while hard water symptoms come back. Push a broom handle down into the salt. If it stops on a hard layer with empty space below, you have a bridge.

How often should a water softener regenerate?

There is no single number. It depends on the system's capacity, your hardness and how much water your home uses. Metered systems regenerate based on actual use, while timer systems follow a schedule you set. Very hard Arizona water usually means more frequent regeneration.

Does a water softener need yearly maintenance?

Yes. Along with monthly salt checks, a yearly look at the brine tank, prefilter, resin, valves and settings keeps the system working. The City of Mesa recommends following the manufacturer's guidance and servicing the unit regularly.

Can I use potassium instead of salt?

Many softeners can use potassium chloride instead of sodium chloride. Check your owner's manual first. If you are on a sodium-restricted diet, the City of Mesa suggests talking with your physician about the added sodium in softened water.

Will a softener protect my tankless water heater?

It removes the hardness minerals that cause scale, which is the main enemy of a tankless unit. Keep the softener maintained, and keep up with tankless water heater descaling as the manufacturer recommends.
