FAQ added (4 questions)(none on the page)
  1. What is the difference between repiping and ePIPE?
    Repiping replaces your water supply lines with new PEX or copper and means opening walls to run it; a whole house repipe usually takes a few days. ePIPE restores the pipes you already have from the inside with an epoxy coating. Our guide to repiping vs relining compares the two.
  2. Is rust-colored water a sign I need to repipe?
    Often. Brown or rusty water usually comes from rust inside galvanized or iron pipe in the home rather than from the city supply. An inspection shows whether the pipes can be cleaned and coated or need replacing.
  3. Do I need to repipe after one leak?
    Not usually. A single leak can be a one-off. Repeat leaks in the same pipe runs mean the pipe is corroding from the inside, and that is when restoring or replacing the whole system makes sense.
  4. Is ePIPE safe for drinking water?
    Yes. The ePIPE coating is NSF/ANSI 61 certified for drinking water, and the pipes are flushed and pressure tested before water service is restored.

FAQ added (4 questions)(none on the page)
  1. How do you get tree roots out of a sewer line?
    Roots are cut out of the line with a mechanical cutter or cleared with hydro jetting. That clears the pipe for a while, but the crack or joint the roots came through is still open, so they grow back unless the pipe is sealed. See how to get rid of tree roots in a sewer line.
  2. Will roots come back after they are cleared?
    Usually, yes. Roots follow the moisture escaping from a cracked pipe or loose joint. Sealing the line from the inside with a liner is what keeps them from coming back.
  3. Can root intrusion be fixed without digging?
    Often, yes. If a camera inspection shows the pipe still holds its shape, the roots are cleared and the line is lined from the inside. A section that has collapsed has to be dug up and replaced first.
  4. Which pipes are most at risk from tree roots?
    Older clay and concrete sewer pipes, because they are made in short sections with many joints that crack or lose their seal over time.

Signs your sewer and drain pipes are outdated

FAQ added (4 questions)(none on the page)
  1. How do I know if my sewer line is old?
    The age of the home and the neighborhood are the first clues, but the only way to know for sure is a sewer camera inspection. It shows the pipe material, its joints and any cracks, roots or sags along the whole line.
  2. Can old sewer pipes be repaired without replacing them?
    Often, yes. If the pipe still holds its shape, it can be cleaned and lined from the inside with a cured-in-place liner. Collapsed or badly sagging sections need to be dug up and replaced. See how to repair a sewer line without digging.
  3. Should I replace an old sewer line before it fails?
    Not necessarily. If a camera inspection shows tight joints and no roots, cracks or sags, there may be nothing to fix yet. The inspection gives you a baseline, so you can watch for changes instead of guessing.
  4. What is the most common sign of an outdated sewer line?
    Slow drains and clogs that keep coming back at several fixtures at once. A single slow sink is usually a local clog; the whole house draining slowly points to the main line.

Cracked vs collapsed sewer pipes

FAQ added (4 questions)(none on the page)
  1. Can a cracked sewer pipe be lined?
    Usually, yes. A cracked pipe still holds its shape, so a cured-in-place liner can be pulled in and cured against the old wall, sealing the cracks from the inside. Pipeliners cures its liners with UV LED light, and the finished liner carries a 50-year life expectancy.
  2. Can a collapsed sewer line be lined?
    Not the collapsed section itself, because a liner needs an open pipe to form against. The usual fix is to dig up and replace the collapsed spot, then line the rest of the line from the inside.
  3. How do I know if my sewer line is cracked or collapsed?
    A sewer camera inspection is the only reliable way. From the surface the symptoms overlap, but the camera shows whether the pipe is cracked and still round or has caved in.
  4. Is a cracked sewer pipe an emergency?
    Not usually, but it gets worse. Cracks let roots in and can wash out the soil around the pipe, which is how a crack turns into a collapse. Frequent backups or sewage in the yard need attention right away.
