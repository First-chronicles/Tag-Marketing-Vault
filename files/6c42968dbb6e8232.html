Quick answer: A tankless vs tank water heater decision comes down to how you weigh upfront cost against long-term efficiency and lifespan. A tank water heater costs less to install and handles big, simultaneous hot-water demands easily, while a tankless unit lasts longer, takes up almost no space, and only heats water when you need it. According to the U.S. Department of Energy, tankless models typically last more than 20 years versus 10 to 15 years for storage tanks, and they avoid the standby energy losses tanks experience. In Arizona, hard water and descaling maintenance are the deciding factors many homeowners overlook.

Choosing between these two systems is one of the biggest plumbing decisions you will make for your East Valley home. Below is a plain-English breakdown so you can pick with confidence, whether you are in Chandler, Gilbert, Mesa, or anywhere across the Phoenix metro.

What is the difference between a tankless vs tank water heater?

The core difference is storage: a tank water heater keeps 20 to 80 gallons of hot water heated and ready around the clock, while a tankless water heater heats water on demand only when a tap or appliance calls for it. The U.S. Department of Energy notes that storage tanks hold a "ready reservoir" of hot water but lose energy continuously through standby heat loss, which is heat escaping the tank into the surrounding garage or closet. A tankless unit, sometimes called a demand-type heater, fires up only when water flows, so it never reheats a standing tank.

That single design choice drives almost every other difference, from monthly energy use to how long the unit lasts.

Which water heater is more energy efficient in Arizona?

A tankless water heater is generally more energy efficient because it eliminates standby heat loss, but the size of the savings depends on how much hot water your household uses. The Department of Energy reports that for homes using 41 gallons or less of hot water per day, demand water heaters can be 24 to 34 percent more energy efficient than conventional storage tanks. For high-use homes around 86 gallons per day, the advantage narrows to roughly 8 to 14 percent.

Arizona adds a wrinkle here. Our incoming water is warmer than in colder states, especially in summer, so any water heater has less work to do to reach your set temperature. That can soften the real-world efficiency gap a little, but the tankless advantage of never reheating a standing tank still holds year-round. There is also no freeze risk to design around here, which simplifies tankless installation compared to northern climates.

How does Arizona hard water affect each type?

Hard water shortens the life of any water heater, but it hits tankless units harder if they are not maintained, because mineral scale builds up inside their narrow heat exchanger. The East Valley sits in some of the hardest water in the country, and that dissolved calcium and magnesium leaves scale behind every time water is heated. In a tank, sediment settles at the bottom and is managed with periodic flushing. In a tankless unit, scale coats the heat exchanger and can choke flow and efficiency if descaling is skipped.

The practical takeaway: a tankless water heater is an excellent choice in Arizona, but plan on regular descaling to protect your investment. Many homeowners here pair a new tankless system with a water softener or scale-reduction setup to slow buildup. Which should you choose?

Choose a tank water heater if upfront cost is your priority or your home has heavy, simultaneous hot-water demand and you do not want to rework your plumbing. Choose a tankless water heater if you value long-term efficiency, a longer lifespan, freed-up floor space, and endless hot water, and you are willing to keep up with descaling. There is no universally "right" answer. The best system is the one that fits your household size, budget, and how you actually use hot water.

Frequently asked questions

Does a tankless water heater really last longer than a tank?

Yes. The U.S. Department of Energy states that most tankless water heaters have a life expectancy of more than 20 years, while storage tank water heaters typically last 10 to 15 years. In hard-water areas like the East Valley, hitting the top of that range depends on regular descaling and maintenance for tankless units and periodic flushing for tanks.

Will a tankless water heater run out of hot water?

No, a tankless water heater does not run out of hot water the way a tank does, because it heats water continuously as it flows. It is limited instead by flow rate, generally 2 to 5 gallons per minute according to the Department of Energy. If too many fixtures run at once, the water may cool, which is why correct sizing for your household matters.

Is tankless worth it with Arizona's hard water?

Tankless can absolutely be worth it in Arizona, as long as you commit to regular descaling. Hard water leaves scale inside the heat exchanger, so units that are flushed on schedule keep their efficiency and long lifespan. Many local homeowners pair a tankless system with a softener or scale-reduction device to reduce buildup.

Which water heater saves more money on energy bills?

A tankless water heater usually saves more on energy because it avoids standby heat loss. The Department of Energy puts the efficiency gain at 24 to 34 percent for lower-use homes and 8 to 14 percent for high-use homes compared with storage tanks. Whether that offsets the higher upfront cost depends on your usage and how long you keep the unit.

Do I need to change my plumbing to switch to tankless?

Sometimes. Switching from a tank to a tankless water heater can require changes to gas lines, venting, or electrical capacity depending on the model and your existing setup.
