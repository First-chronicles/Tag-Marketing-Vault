How often should you flush a tankless water heater in Arizona?

Once a year is the baseline we recommend for tankless water heaters in the East Valley, and most homeowners follow a 12-month schedule. A yearly descaling flush clears the scale that has built up before it starts to choke flow and efficiency.

Think of one year as the longest you should go, not a target to stretch. The right schedule for your home depends on how hard your water is, how much hot water your household uses, and whether you have a water softener. A busy family running several showers a day pushes more heated water through the unit than a couple in a small condo, so scale collects faster.

Your model's maintenance instructions matter too, so keep the owner's manual handy or look it up by model number. If you are not sure what fits, a licensed plumber can look at your unit and your water and recommend a schedule.

Why does East Valley hard water matter so much for a tankless unit?

Hard water matters because a tankless unit heats water as it flows through a compact heat exchanger, and the minerals in that water leave scale on the hot surfaces. That scale is the root of most tankless water heater hard water problems. TheU.S. Geological Survey explains that heated hard water can form solid calcium carbonate deposits, which can reduce the life of equipment, raise the cost of heating water, and clog pipes.

The East Valley has plenty of those minerals. The City of Mesa's 2025 water quality report lists hardness of about 12 grains per gallon in its City Zone and about 16 grains per gallon in its Eastern and Southern zone. The Town of Queen Creek reports 6 to 17 grains per gallon. For reference, USGS classifies water above 180 milligrams per liter, roughly 10.5 grains per gallon, as very hard.

In a traditional tank, minerals mostly settle to the bottom as sediment. In a tankless unit, scale coats the narrow passages where water picks up heat, so buildup has a direct effect on flow and temperature. Still weighing the two systems? Does Arizona's heat change how fast scale builds up?

No, warm weather does not protect a tankless water heater from scale. In summer, the water coming into your home is warmer, so the unit has less heating to do to reach your set temperature. Minerals still come out of the water whenever it is heated, though, so scale keeps forming in every season.

Your temperature setting matters more than the weather. Hotter water generally leaves more scale behind, so running the unit hotter than your household needs can add to buildup. If your family is comfortable at a lower setting, ask your plumber whether a change makes sense.

What are the signs your tankless water heater needs descaling?

The most common sign is hot water that is less steady or less hot than it used to be. Watch for these warning signs:

  • Water temperature that swings between hot and lukewarm during a shower
  • Hot water that takes longer to arrive or feels weaker at hot taps
  • An error code on the unit's display
  • The unit shutting off in the middle of use
  • Unusual noises from the unit while it heats
  • Higher gas or electric bills with no change in how you use hot water
  • White, crusty buildup on showerheads and faucet aerators, a clue that your water is leaving scale inside appliances too

If you notice any of these and it has been a year or more since the last flush, descaling is a sensible first step. If problems continue after a flush, the cause may be something else, such as a sizing, gas supply, or venting issue, or a part that needs repair. What does tankless water heater descaling involve?

A flush circulates a descaling solution, usually white vinegar or a commercial descaler, through the heat exchanger to dissolve scale, then rinses the unit with clean water. First the unit is shut down and closed off using isolation valves, often called service valves, on its hot and cold water lines. A small pump and hoses connect to those valves so the solution can cycle through.

A full tankless water heater maintenance visit goes beyond the flush. It usually includes cleaning the inlet filter screen, which catches grit before it enters the unit, and cleaning the flame rod on gas models. It is also a good time to check for small leaks and confirm the unit runs correctly afterward.

Some installations have no service valves at all, which makes flushing much harder. In that case, a plumber can add them so future maintenance is simple.

Should you descale a tankless water heater yourself or call a plumber?

It depends on the unit. A handy homeowner with an electric tankless water heater, service valves already in place, and the manufacturer's instructions may be comfortable doing it. For gas units, or any setup without service valves, a licensed plumber is the safer choice.

Caution matters here. Gas units involve shutting off and restoring the gas supply and confirming the unit fires and vents properly, and mistakes with gas or venting are a real safety hazard. Electric units need power fully shut off at the breaker before anyone opens them. Water in the unit can be hot enough to scald, and used descaling solution needs to be drained properly. Loosening the wrong connection can also cause a leak.

Does a water softener help protect a tankless water heater?

Yes, a water softener slows scale buildup by removing hardness minerals before water reaches your water heater, which helps keep the heat exchanger cleaner between flushes. In the harder parts of the East Valley, that can mean less scale to clear at each service.

A softener does not replace maintenance, though. The inlet filter still collects debris, and a yearly check still catches small problems early. If you want to protect a tankless unit from hard water, learn more about our water softener services.

Scale keeps building up inside the heat exchanger, and the unit slowly loses efficiency. Over time that can lead to temperature swings, error codes, and shutdowns, and heavy buildup can eventually damage the unit.
