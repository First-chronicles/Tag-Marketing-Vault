What Is a Sewer Scope Inspection and How Does It Work?

A sewer scope inspection is a video check of your main sewer line. That's the pipe that runs under the yard from the house to the city sewer in the street or alley. Every sink, shower, toilet, and washer in the house drains into it.

Here's how it works:

  1. The technician finds a way in. Most homes have a cleanout. That's a short pipe with a cap, usually outside near the house, that gives direct access to the sewer line.
  2. The camera goes down the pipe. A small waterproof camera on a long, bendable cable is pushed through the line toward the street.
  3. The video records everything. The technician watches a screen as the camera moves. The cable shows how far the camera has traveled, so any problem can be marked by its distance from the house.
  4. You get the results. A good inspection ends with the video and a plain summary of what was found and where.

Nothing gets dug up. The whole check happens from inside the pipe.

What Can a Sewer Scope Find?

A sewer line can look fine from the outside and still be in bad shape. The camera shows what's really going on inside.

Is a Sewer Scope Part of a Home Inspection?

Usually, no. A standard home inspection covers the plumbing the inspector can see, like faucets, drains, and exposed pipes. The main sewer line is buried, so the inspector can't see inside it.

Some home inspectors offer a sewer scope as an add-on. You can also hire a plumber or a sewer company to do it. Either way, ask for it by name. Don't assume it's included.

Should You Get a Sewer Scope When Buying a House?

Yes, for most homes. Here's why it's worth it.

You become responsible for the pipe. The City of Phoenix says the property owner is responsible for the sewer line from the house to the connection with the city main. The city only repairs broken lines in the public right of way. Once you close, a problem in that pipe is yours. Check with your city or HOA if you're buying outside Phoenix or in a condo.

Sewer problems are hidden. A house can pass a home inspection and still have roots or a crack in the sewer line. The drains might seem fine during a short showing, especially if the home has been empty.

Older homes carry more risk. Older homes are more likely to have clay sewer pipe or cast iron. Clay joints let roots in, and cast iron rusts from the inside. Here's how long cast iron pipes last. Newer homes usually have plastic pipe, but that pipe can still sag, shift, or get damaged.

It gives you time to act. If the camera finds a problem during your inspection period, you still have options. Many buyers use the video to ask the seller for a repair or a credit before closing.

A sewer scope is a good idea even for a newer home. It matters most when:

  • the house is older
  • there are large trees near the sewer line
  • the home sat empty for a while
  • you see slow drains, gurgling, or a sewage smell
  • the seller says the line was "recently cleared"

What Should You Ask For After a Sewer Scope?

Don't settle for a quick "looks fine." Ask for:

  • The video. It's your proof, and other contractors can review it.
  • A written summary. It should list each problem and how far it is from the house.
  • The pipe material. Clay, cast iron, and plastic each have different weak spots.
  • The full length. Ask whether the camera reached the city connection. If it stopped early, ask why.
  • A clear next step. Is it fine, does it need cleaning, or does it need repair?

What If the Sewer Scope Finds a Problem?

A problem found before closing is much easier to deal with than one found after you move in. The fix depends on what the camera shows.

  • Buildup or minor roots: Often a cleaning job. Hydro jetting uses high-pressure water to clear the pipe walls.
  • Cracks, root entry points, or separated joints: These can often be fixed by lining the pipe. A new pipe is formed inside the old one, so no trench goes across the yard.
  • A collapse or a deep belly: These may need a different repair. The video helps show which one.

Sewer Scope Inspection FAQs

How long does a sewer scope inspection take?

Most scopes take about an hour at the house. It can take longer if the cleanout is hard to find or the line has to be cleared before the camera can get through.

How often should a sewer scope be done?

There's no set schedule. Get one before you buy a home, when drains back up or keep clogging, or when roots have been a problem before. For an older home with a known issue, a check every few years helps you catch changes early.

What is the difference between a septic inspection and a sewer scope?

A sewer scope checks the pipe that runs from the house to the city sewer. A septic inspection is for homes that aren't on city sewer. It checks the septic tank and the area where the water drains into the ground. Find out which one the house has before you book.

Do plumbers do sewer scopes?

Yes. Many plumbers and sewer repair companies do sewer camera inspections, and some home inspectors offer them as an add-on. A sewer company can also tell you what a repair would involve.

Is it worth doing a sewer scope?

For most buyers, yes. It's a small step compared to the cost of finding a sewer problem after you own the home.

Can I do a sewer scope myself?

You can rent a basic drain camera, but reading the video takes practice. Roots, sags, and shifted joints are easy to miss. When you're buying a home, a professional report carries more weight with the seller.
